Module 01

Introduction to Biostatistics

Understanding the role of statistics in medicine, healthcare, clinical practice and health research.

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Define biostatistics and explain its importance in medicine.
  • Distinguish between descriptive and inferential statistics.
  • Explain how statistics supports clinical decision-making.
  • Identify common applications of biostatistics in healthcare.
  • Explain the relationship between populations, samples and statistical inference.
  • Recognize the importance of data quality in clinical research.

1. Introduction to Biostatistics

Healthcare professionals make decisions every day using information collected from patients, communities and health systems.

For example, a clinician may want to know whether a patient's blood pressure has improved after treatment. A researcher may want to determine whether a new treatment is more effective than the existing treatment. A public health professional may want to estimate the prevalence of a disease in a population.

All of these questions involve data. Biostatistics provides the methods used to collect, organize, analyze, interpret and communicate health-related data.

Definition:

Biostatistics is the application of statistical methods to biological, medical and health-related data.

2. Why Is Biostatistics Important in Clinical Medicine?

Biostatistics is not simply about calculating numbers. It helps clinicians and researchers convert data into meaningful evidence.

Biostatistics helps us to:

  • Understand patterns of disease.
  • Measure disease frequency and burden.
  • Evaluate treatments and interventions.
  • Compare groups of patients.
  • Assess risk factors for disease.
  • Interpret clinical research findings.
  • Make evidence-based clinical decisions.
🏥 Clinical Example

Suppose a hospital introduces a new protocol for managing patients with hypertension.

After six months, the hospital wants to determine whether the protocol improved blood pressure control.

Biostatistics can help the research team compare blood pressure measurements before and after implementation and determine whether the observed change is likely to represent a real improvement.

3. Descriptive and Inferential Statistics

Statistics can broadly be divided into two major areas: descriptive statistics and inferential statistics.

Descriptive Statistics Inferential Statistics
Summarizes and describes collected data. Uses sample data to make conclusions about a population.
Examples include mean, median, percentage and standard deviation. Examples include confidence intervals, hypothesis tests and regression.
Answers: "What does our data look like?" Answers: "What can we conclude beyond our sample?"

4. Population and Sample

One of the most important concepts in biostatistics is the distinction between a population and a sample.

Population

The complete group of individuals or observations that we are interested in studying.

Sample

A subset of the population selected for study.

Example

Suppose we want to estimate the prevalence of hypertension among adults in Kisumu County.

The population could be all adults living in Kisumu County, while the sample could consist of 1,000 adults selected for the study.

5. From Clinical Data to Evidence

Biostatistical analysis is part of a larger process. A typical clinical research process may follow these steps:

  1. Identify a clinical or public health question.
  2. Design the study.
  3. Collect appropriate data.
  4. Clean and organize the data.
  5. Describe the data.
  6. Conduct statistical analysis.
  7. Interpret the findings.
  8. Communicate the evidence.
  9. Use the evidence to inform decisions.
Key idea:

Good statistical analysis cannot compensate for poor study design or poor-quality data.

6. Clinical Application

Consider the following clinical research question:

Research Question

Does a new medication reduce systolic blood pressure more effectively than the standard medication among adults with hypertension?

To answer this question, researchers may need to:

  • Define the study population.
  • Select an appropriate sample.
  • Measure systolic blood pressure.
  • Record treatment assignment.
  • Summarize the data.
  • Compare the treatment groups.
  • Estimate the difference in blood pressure.
  • Determine the uncertainty around the estimate.
  • Interpret the clinical significance of the findings.

These activities demonstrate why biostatistics is fundamental to evidence-based medicine.
